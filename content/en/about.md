## Glia and agora: the name

Glia are the brain's other cells. They surround neurons, insulate their fibres, clear away waste and help shape the connections between them. Their name comes from the Greek word for glue, from a time when they were thought simply to hold neurons in place. Astrocytes, one kind of glia, signal to each other through waves of calcium.

The agora was the open square at the centre of an ancient Greek city, where people met to trade, talk and decide together. Gliagora joins the two words: a meeting place for neuroscience that, like glia, works in the background and links what would otherwise stay apart. Our logo shows the same idea: a calcium wave spreading from an astrocyte, drawn as a G.

## Who Gliagora is for

Gliagora is for students and early-career neuroscientists looking for their next position, school or grant: MSc and PhD applicants, PhD candidates and postdocs. It is also for researchers who hire or follow funding calls, clinicians interested in how research reaches the clinic, and anyone curious about the brain.

## How curation works

1. **Discover.** We follow employers' career pages, funders, scientific societies, journals and preprint servers, and we read the suggestions people send us.
2. **Verify on the original page.** A deadline or condition is published only after we have seen it on the original page. Every entry shows when it was last checked.
3. **Summarise in our own words.** Each entry gives the facts and a short summary we write ourselves. We do not copy descriptions, abstracts, images or logos.
4. **Tag.** One shared set of tags (subfield, method, model system, career stage and place) connects all six windows.
5. **Link to the source.** You apply, register or read on the original site. Gliagora does not take applications, charge fees or act as an intermediary.
6. **Expire automatically.** Closed positions and past events leave the lists on their own.

## Sources and editorial policy

We list positions published by the employers themselves, such as universities, institutes and companies, and not advertisements from recruitment agencies. Job boards help us find positions, but we always link to the employer's own page. Papers of the month are chosen from openly licensed papers (CC BY, CC BY-SA or CC0); now and then we may add another paper as our own summary with a link to it. An AI assistant helps us: it drafts each position's summary and checks each position against our written rules. Positions are published only when our editor starts a publishing round, and a person does not check every position. Our editor is responsible for every listing and corrects or removes any of them as soon as a problem is found. There are no paid placements. The full sources and editorial policy will be published before the site opens.

## Reading employers' pages

To find new positions sooner, a program of ours reads some employers' own vacancy pages and feeds: those of universities, institutes and companies, never job boards. It names itself "Gliagora-Monitor" and links to this section. It reads each list at most once a day, makes one request at a time to a site, follows the site's `robots.txt` and its terms of use, and leaves alone any site whose terms forbid automated reading. It keeps only what we need to review a position: its address, its title, the facts we check (such as the deadline) and its text with names, email addresses and phone numbers removed, until 30 days after we have decided on it. It publishes nothing by itself. Positions are published only when our editor starts a publishing round: an AI assistant then checks each position against our written rules, and the program reads the employer's page once more just before publishing.

If you run one of these sites and would rather we did not read it, block `Gliagora-Monitor` in your `robots.txt` or write to [hello@gliagora.com](mailto:hello@gliagora.com), and we will stop.
